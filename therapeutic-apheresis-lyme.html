Quick answer

What is therapeutic apheresis for Lyme disease?

Therapeutic apheresis is a medical blood-filtering procedure: blood is drawn, passed through a filter that separates out a target fraction of the plasma, and then returned to the body. For chronic Lyme the version typically used is a membrane-based double-filtration plasmapheresis (one branded system is INUSpheresis), designed to remove larger inflammatory molecules while keeping the proteins the body needs. Clinics that use it for Lyme describe it as clearing an accumulated inflammatory load — inflammatory lipids, immune complexes, autoantibodies, cytokines, and pathogen- and mold-derived debris — so that immune-recalibrating treatments land on a cleaner internal environment. It does not kill the Lyme bacteria directly. Apheresis is well established in mainstream medicine for certain other conditions, but its use for chronic Lyme is emerging and not an FDA-approved standard of care; the published Lyme data is small and uncontrolled, and candidacy is assessed by a medical team.

What is therapeutic apheresis?

"Apheresis" simply means to take away. In practice, therapeutic apheresis is a procedure where your blood is drawn through a line, circulated through a device that separates it into components and filters out a specific target fraction, and then returned to your body. It's the same broad family of technology used in established medicine to treat certain neurological, kidney, and blood disorders — so the procedure itself is not fringe, even though its use for chronic Lyme is newer.

The version most often used in chronic illness is double-filtration plasmapheresis (DFPP) — sometimes delivered through a branded system such as INUSpheresis. A first filter separates the plasma from your blood cells; a second, finer membrane then sieves out the larger molecules while letting the smaller proteins your body needs (like albumin) pass back through. The cleaned plasma is recombined with your cells and returned to you. Unlike a transfusion, nothing donor-derived is added; unlike dialysis, the goal isn't to replace kidney function but to lift out a specific inflammatory burden.

What it filters from the blood

Here's where I want to be careful and precise, because this is where marketing and evidence often blur. A double-filtration membrane is designed to remove larger, high-molecular-weight substances from plasma while returning the smaller proteins. In chronic illness, the targets the clinics describe include:

  • Inflammatory lipids — such as oxidized LDL and lipoprotein(a), which are elevated in many chronic-Lyme patients. This is the part with the most actual measurement behind it.
  • Immune complexes and autoantibodies — the clumped antibody-antigen debris and self-directed antibodies that can keep inflammation and autoimmune-type symptoms running.
  • Cytokines and inflammatory signaling molecules — the chemical "alarm bells" that keep the immune system in overdrive.
  • Mold toxins and pathogen-derived debris — mycotoxins and microbial byproducts that add to the body's total load.

An honest flag: the strongest published measurements in Lyme patients are the reductions in inflammatory lipids. The broader claims — clearing cytokines, toxins, heavy metals — come largely from clinic and device-maker descriptions rather than controlled trials. That doesn't make them wrong, but it does make them rationale rather than proof, and you deserve to know which is which.

What the evidence shows (and doesn't)

The honest state of it: therapeutic apheresis is a well-established, evidence-based procedure in mainstream medicine — but for a defined list of other conditions, not chronic Lyme. For Lyme specifically, there are no large randomized controlled trials. Here's what does and doesn't exist:

  • The procedure itself is proven technology. Therapeutic plasma exchange and related apheresis methods are standard, physician-governed treatments for certain autoimmune, neurological, and metabolic disorders. The machinery and safety profile are not experimental.
  • The Lyme data is real but limited. The most-cited work is a retrospective series of several hundred patients with a Lyme history, reporting reduced inflammatory lipids (including lipoprotein(a)) and improved symptoms after double-filtration apheresis. That's a meaningful signal — but it's uncontrolled, it leans on lipid measurements, and retrospective series can't prove cause and effect the way a randomized trial can.
  • What's missing is controlled Lyme trials. No one has yet run a large, randomized study showing apheresis changes the course of chronic Lyme on its own. That's the honest gap, and it's exactly why it's used as one preparatory part of a plan, not a stand-alone treatment.

So: biologically reasonable, supported by established use elsewhere and by early Lyme data, and — in my family's and many others' lived experience — genuinely helpful as a clearing step. But not a proven cure, and anyone who frames it as one is getting ahead of the evidence.

What a session is actually like

If the word "apheresis" sounds frightening, the reality is calmer than you'd expect. A session looks a lot like an extended IV or blood-donation visit: a line is placed, your blood circulates slowly through the filtering device over roughly a couple of hours, and you rest — often reading, dozing, or talking with the staff — while it runs. You're monitored throughout. Many people feel noticeably lighter and clearer in the days afterward, though a tired or "detox-y" stretch first is common as the body adjusts.

Because it's an invasive procedure, it carries real risks — a drop in blood pressure, bleeding or clotting, reactions to the anticoagulant used to keep the circuit flowing, and line-related issues — which is exactly why it belongs in a monitored medical setting and isn't right for everyone.

Who it's for — and who it isn't

Apheresis tends to be considered for people with chronic Lyme and tick-borne co-infections who carry a heavy inflammatory load — especially when the immune system is clearly overreacting rather than fighting cleanly. Because the same pattern of inflammatory burden shows up elsewhere, people with mold illness / CIRS, long COVID, or chronic EBV are sometimes considered too.

But it isn't universal. It asks something of the cardiovascular system, and people with certain heart, vascular, bleeding, or other conditions may not be candidates — or may need a modified approach. Pregnancy and some medications matter as well. This is a decision for a medical team that has reviewed your full history, not a forum and not me. If a program ever waves off that screening, treat it as a red flag.
